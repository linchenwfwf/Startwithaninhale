Conjunctive service · DBT skills

DBT skills for when feelings arrive fast and big

One-on-one emotional regulation skills for neurodivergent adults, alongside the care you already have.

Maybe you go from fine to flooded in seconds. Maybe you hold it together all day and fall apart at home, or you can't say what you're feeling until hours later. Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT) offers a concrete set of skills for these moments. I teach them with neurodivergent nervous systems in mind, as an add-on to the therapy you already have.

Your therapist suggested it

Your therapy is going well, but you need more concrete tools for the hard moments.

Neurodivergent-affirming

DBT that doesn't ask you to mask

Standard DBT materials weren't written with most neurodivergent adults in mind. As someone who specializes in this work, I change how skills are taught and what they're for.

Regulation, not compliance

The goal isn't to appear calm for other people. It's for you to feel more steady and to have more choice.

Meltdowns aren't bad behavior

Meltdowns and shutdowns are often overload. We look at sensory load, demands, and fatigue first, and fix the environment before asking you to cope better.

Body first

If emotions are hard to name, we begin with sensation, using interoception practices. A skill you can't feel coming is hard to use.

Sensory swaps

A skill should suit your senses. Cold water might be perfect or unbearable. Weighted blankets, stimming, and movement all count.

Concrete language

If “wise mind” feels vague, we define it in your terms, with examples, written steps, and visuals you can use when you're flooded.

Scripts, not eye contact

Interpersonal skills here are about getting your needs met, not performing neurotypical conversation. Texting your request is a valid choice.

The skills

The four areas of DBT skills

We won't go through a workbook in order. We start with what you need most, and we practice it until it's there when you need it. These skills come from Marsha Linehan's DBT Skills Training Manual.

  • Mindfulness
  • Distress tolerance
  • Emotion regulation
  • Interpersonal effectiveness

Mindfulness: knowing what's happening

This is the base for the other skills. For neurodivergent adults, it isn't about sitting still. It's about noticing.

Wise mind

Finding the place between pure emotion and pure logic, where you know what matters and what's true right now.

Observe, describe, participate

Notice what's happening, put plain words to it, and join in without watching yourself from the outside.

One thing at a time

Doing just the thing in front of you, on purpose.

Non-judgmentally

“I'm late and my heart is racing” instead of “I'm a disaster.” This helps a lot with shame.

Distress tolerance: surviving the moment

For when it's too much and you can't fix it right now. The aim is to get through without making things worse.

STOP

Stop, Take a step back, Observe, and Proceed mindfully. It's a brake for the moment before you act.

TIPP

Temperature, Intense movement, Paced breathing, and Paired muscle relaxation can calm your body quickly. Check with a doctor before using cold water if you have a heart condition or an eating disorder.

Pros and cons

Weighing what happens if you act on the urge against what happens if you don't, ideally before the moment comes.

ACCEPTS

Short-term distraction through activities, helping others, comparisons, other emotions, pushing away, thoughts, and sensations.

Self-soothe with the senses

Comfort through sight, sound, smell, taste, and touch. We build a kit that fits your own sensory profile.

IMPROVE the moment

Using imagery, meaning, prayer or values, relaxation, one thing at a time, a brief vacation, and encouragement.

Radical acceptance

Stop fighting what has already happened. This doesn't mean approving of it. It means no longer adding suffering on top of pain.

Emotion regulation: changing the pattern

For the long game, so that emotions come less often, less intensely, and with more understanding.

Name it

Learning what emotions feel like in your body, and telling them apart. We can start from sensation if words don't come.

Check the facts

Asking whether your emotion fits the situation, and how intense it should be. This pairs well with rejection sensitivity.

Opposite action

When an emotion doesn't fit the facts, doing the opposite of its urge: approaching instead of hiding, for instance.

Problem solving

When the emotion does fit the facts, working on what's causing it.

Build positive experiences

Scheduling pleasant things on purpose, and building toward goals that matter to you.

Cope ahead

Rehearsing a hard situation before it comes: the family dinner, the performance review, the crowded party.

PLEASE

Reducing vulnerability by treating illness, eating, avoiding mood-altering substances, sleeping, and moving. We adapt this to your energy.

Interpersonal effectiveness: staying yourself with others

For asking, refusing, and keeping relationships and self-respect at the same time.

DEAR MAN

A structure for asking or saying no: Describe, Express, Assert, Reinforce, stay Mindful, Appear confident, Negotiate. Written drafts are welcome.

GIVE

Keeping the relationship: be Gentle, act Interested, Validate, use an Easy manner.

FAST

Keeping your self-respect: be Fair, no extra Apologies, Stick to your values, be Truthful.

Validation

Learning to say “that makes sense” to others and to yourself, including about things you've been told are overreactions.

How it works

From the workbook to real life

  1. Map your pattern. What sets it off, how it builds in your body, and what you do next. We also map sensory triggers and burnout signs.
  2. Pick a skill. We choose one or two that fit your pattern, and adapt them to your sensory needs.
  3. Practice while calm. A skill you've only read about won't show up when you're flooded. We rehearse in session.
  4. Make it easy to reach. Cards on your phone, a sensory kit, a written script.
  5. Review what happened. After a hard moment, we go through it with curiosity. Which link in the chain could go differently next time?
What this is, and isn't. Comprehensive DBT includes individual therapy, a skills group, between-session phone coaching, and a therapist consultation team. This service is DBT-informed individual skills training, offered as an add-on. It doesn't include crisis phone coaching. If you've recently attempted suicide or have frequent self-harm, a comprehensive DBT program or a higher level of care is the safer place to start, and I'll help you look for one.

Questions

Common questions

What does conjunctive mean?

Conjunctive (also called adjunctive) means this work runs alongside the care you already have. Your therapist stays your therapist. I add one focused piece they may not offer, and with your written permission we can stay in touch so the work fits together.

Does DBT work for neurodivergent adults?

Research is growing but still young. A randomized trial of comprehensive DBT in autistic adults with suicidal thoughts and behaviors found it promising and safe, and its authors call for replication. DBT-based skills groups have also been studied in adults with ADHD, with mixed results. Individual, adapted skills work like this hasn't been tested the same way, so we track what's helping you and change what isn't.

Do I need a borderline personality disorder diagnosis?

No. DBT was first developed for people with chronic suicidality and BPD, but the skills are now used much more broadly. You don't need any particular diagnosis to learn them.

How is this different from a DBT skills group?

You work one-on-one, at your own pace, in your own language. Sessions can focus on what's happening in your life and your sensory needs. A group offers community and peer practice, and some people choose it for that.

What if I'm in crisis between sessions?

This practice isn't an emergency service. Call or text 988 at any time, or call 911. Part of our work is writing a plan for hard moments before you need one.
