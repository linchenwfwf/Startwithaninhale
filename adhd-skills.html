How it works

Therapy for the practical part

Conjunctive means this works alongside your existing care. Your therapist keeps doing the deeper work. Your prescriber keeps managing medication. Our sessions focus on one thing: turning what you know about yourself into what you actually do.

  1. We map your real life. We look at your week and where things go sideways, plus what has already worked and why you stopped using it.
  2. We pick one or two targets. Not everything. Maybe mornings, or email, or the bills.
  3. We build it together. We set up the calendar, write the reminder, or start the dreaded task right there in session.
  4. You try it out. You test it in your week as an experiment. If it fails, that's useful information, not a character flaw.
  5. We adjust. We keep what works, change what doesn't, and plan for the moment the novelty wears off.

Neurodivergent-affirming

The goal isn't to act neurotypical

Built around you

I won't hand you a planner made for someone else's brain. We start from your strengths, your interests, and how you already get things done.

If you're AuDHD

Routine and novelty can pull in opposite directions. We design for both, including your sensory needs and your need for recovery time.

Burnout comes first

If you're running on empty, a productivity system won't fix it. Sometimes the right skill is doing less, and we'll work out what to let go of.

Culture and family

Many of my clients grew up with high expectations and little language for ADHD. We can talk about that too, in English or 普通话.

Getting started

Starting is often the hardest part. The block isn't laziness, and the task doesn't need more willpower. It needs a smaller, clearer way in.

Shrink the time

Pick the version that fits your energy: 30 seconds, 3 minutes, 13 minutes, or 30 minutes. Each one counts as a win on its own.

Name the next physical action

“Do taxes” can't be started. “Open the folder and find last year's return” can. If a step still feels heavy, make it smaller.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

The five-minute agreement

Agree to work for five minutes, with full permission to stop afterward. You often keep going, and stopping still counts.

Eat the ice cream

Skip “eat the frog.” Begin with something small or enjoyable, then ride that momentum into the harder task.

If-then plans

Decide the moment ahead of time: “When I put my coffee down, I will open the report.” Researchers call these implementation intentions.

Body doubling

Work beside someone: a friend on video, a person at the next table, a virtual coworking room. They don't help, they're just there.

A launch ritual

Use the same small sequence every time: the same playlist, the same seat, the same drink. It tells your brain work is beginning.

Start in the middle

You don't have to begin at the beginning. Write the easy paragraph or fill in the section you already know.

Time and time blindness

Many ADHD adults experience two kinds of time: now, and not now. These tools make time something you can see.

Visual timers

Use a timer you can watch count down, and clocks in every room you use, including the bathroom.

Estimate, then check

Guess how long a task takes, time it, and compare. A few weeks of this gives you real data about your own speed.

Plan backward

Start from the moment you need to arrive and count back through travel, shoes, keys, and shower. Then add a buffer.

Time blocks with margins

Put tasks on the calendar as blocks, including transition time between them. A block with no margin is a wish.

Transition alarms

Set one alarm to wrap up and one to leave. Label each with what comes next, like “put on shoes.”

Exit plans for hyperfocus

Before diving in, set an alarm across the room and decide what you'll do when it rings: stand, drink water, check the plan.

Replace “later”

If you catch yourself saying “later,” attach a time and put it somewhere you'll see it.

Planning and priorities

A simple system you actually use beats a beautiful one you abandon by Thursday.

One calendar, one list

Keep every appointment in one calendar and every task in one list, and look at both daily.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

A five-minute daily plan

Same time, same place. Look at today, then choose what actually fits. A morning coffee or a bedtime alarm can anchor it.

Rate your tasks

Sort tasks by importance and urgency so the loudest item doesn't automatically win.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Three things

Choose up to three items for today. Everything else waits on the list, out of sight of today's plan.

A done list

Write down what you did, including what wasn't planned. ADHD brains often remember only what's left.

Weekly reset

Once a week, clear your inbox, move unfinished tasks, and look at the week ahead. Body doubling helps here too.

Pick a plan, then problem-solve

When you're stuck between options, list them, weigh them quickly, and pick one to try. A decent choice beats a perfect one you never make.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Memory and object impermanence

Out of sight can really mean out of mind. Stop relying on memory and let your environment do the remembering. Russell Barkley calls this putting the help at the point of performance.

Make it visual

Leave what you need where you'll trip over it: on the counter, by the door, on your shoes.

One capture spot

Keep one notes app or pocket notebook, and write things down the moment they come up.

A home for key items

Keys, wallet, and meds each get one spot, near where you actually use them.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Clear bins, open shelves

If you can't see it, you may forget you own it. Labels and transparent containers help. So do fridge shelves at eye level.

Location-based reminders

Set your phone to remind you when you get home or arrive at the pharmacy, rather than at a random time.

Leaving-the-house check

Post a short list on the door, like phone, keys, wallet, meds. Bluetooth trackers help with what still wanders.

People list

Keep a note of who you meant to reply to and what you wanted to say. Caring about people doesn't always mean remembering to text.

Focus and distraction

The goal isn't constant attention. It's knowing your own attention well enough to plan around it.

Distractibility delay

When a thought pulls you away, jot it down for later and go back to the task. It's saved, so your brain can let it go.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Measure your attention span

Time how long you can focus on a boring task before drifting, then break work into chunks of that length.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Scheduled breaks

Decide on your breaks ahead of time and what they include. Twenty-five minutes on, five off is one version; yours might differ.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Cue yourself back

Use a repeating alarm or a colored dot on your desk as a prompt to ask, “Am I on what I meant to be doing?”

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Shape the room

Put your phone in another room, use site blockers, close extra tabs, and try the noise level that works: silence, brown noise, or a café.

Move while you work

Walk during calls, stand at a counter, or use a fidget. Movement can help attention rather than compete with it.

Motivation

Importance alone rarely gets an ADHD brain moving. Interest, novelty, challenge, and urgency do. So we build those in on purpose.

Pair the boring with the good

Your favorite show plays only while you fold laundry. Researchers call this temptation bundling.

Borrowed deadlines

Tell someone when you'll send it, book the review meeting, or invite a friend over so the apartment gets cleaned.

Make it a game

Race a timer, beat yesterday's number, or see how much you can finish in one song.

Reward right away

A reward next month doesn't reach today's brain. Pick something small that happens immediately after.

Rotate your systems

When an app stops working, it may just have stopped being new. Switching isn't failure. Plan for it.

Connect it to why

Say out loud what the task protects: your rent, your kid's weekend, your own peace of mind.

Emotions, shame, and self-talk

ADHD isn't only about tasks. Many adults arrive carrying years of being told they were too much or not enough.

Catch the thought

Notice all-or-nothing thinking (“I ruined the whole day”) and test it. CBT also works on overly optimistic thoughts, like “this will only take ten minutes.”

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Pause before responding

Take a breath, name the feeling, and walk away for a moment if you need to. Save the hard reply as a draft.

Check the sting

When a slow reply lands hard, list what you know and what you're guessing. Rejection sensitivity feels like fact, but it may not be.

Three kinds of days

Keep a minimum, average, and fuller version of your routine, so there's always one that fits your capacity.

Shame-free review

Replace “what's wrong with me?” with “what got in the way, and what would make it easier next time?”

Impulsivity, money, and decisions

Put a little space between the urge and the action, and make the easy path the safe one.

A 24-hour rule

Leave it in the cart overnight. If you still want it tomorrow, decide then.

Add friction

Remove saved cards, delete shopping apps, and sign out of the sites that catch you.

Automate what you can

Set up autopay, automatic transfers, and recurring orders, so that fewer things depend on remembering.

A mail and paperwork system

Use one tray and one recurring time to go through it. Sort papers and bills into a small number of clearly labeled files.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

A yes-pause

Practice “let me check and get back to you” before committing your time.

Home and daily living

Home tasks have no deadline and no audience, which makes them tough for ADHD. Lower the bar and make the path shorter.

A ten-minute reset

Put on a playlist, tidy until it ends, and stop. It doesn't need to be finished.

The doom box

When a pile overwhelms you, put it all in one box and sort it later. The room is clear, and nothing is lost.

Put things where you drop them

If the bag always lands on the chair, put a hook by the chair. Change the system, not yourself.

Default meals

Keep three meals you can make on autopilot and easy food for bad days. Food matters more than how good it looks.

Reduce the steps

Skip folding and use bins. Keep a second set of supplies where they're used. If it works, it's a valid system.

Work and relationships

ADHD shows up with other people too. We work on the practical side and the repair.

Written recaps

After a meeting, send a two-line summary of what you heard and what's next. It works as memory, and it shows follow-through.

Note it, don't interrupt

Jot down your thought while the other person finishes. You won't lose it, and you can keep listening.

Templates for replies

Save a few short messages, so a text never has to be perfect to get sent.

Scripts for repair

“I forgot, and I'm sorry. Here's what I'm doing about it.” A clear repair can do more than a long apology.

Accommodations at work

If you want them, we can think through what to ask for, such as written instructions, quieter space, or flexible deadlines.

Bring in a partner

If you'd like, a partner can join a session to learn about ADHD and agree on how to help without becoming your manager.

Used in research-tested CBT for adult ADHD

Body and energy

Attention rests on the body. Sleep, food, and movement all affect executive function.

A bedtime alarm

Set an alarm for starting to wind down, not just for waking up. Bedtime can slip past unnoticed otherwise.

Movement as a tool

John Ratey's work points to exercise as a support for attention and mood. A short walk counts.

Food reminders

Hyperfocus and some medications can blunt hunger. Set an alarm and keep easy protein nearby.

Medication routines

If you take medication, tie it to something you already do. Questions about dose or timing go to your prescriber.

Check in with your body

One slow breath, a look around the room, your feet on the floor. A few seconds of grounding can reset an overloaded system.

Questions

Common questions

What does conjunctive mean?

Conjunctive (also called adjunctive) means this work runs alongside the care you already have. Your therapist stays your therapist. I add one focused piece they may not offer, and with your written permission we can stay in touch so the work fits together.

Do I need a formal ADHD diagnosis?

Tell me where you are in that process during our consultation. If you suspect ADHD but haven't been evaluated, we can talk through what makes sense, including where to seek an evaluation. I don't prescribe medication.

Is this ADHD coaching?

It's therapy, with a practical focus. Like a coach, I help with systems and follow-through. As a licensed clinician, I can also work with the anxiety, shame, and history that get in the way.

Do these skills replace medication?

No. Skills and medication do different jobs, and many people use both. Some of the best-known trials of CBT for adult ADHD were done with adults who were already taking medication and still had symptoms. Any decisions about medication belong with you and your prescriber.

Will my therapist mind?

Most therapists welcome this kind of support, and it's a good idea to mention it to them. With your written permission, I can coordinate with them so we aren't working at cross purposes.
