Understanding ADHD

A brain wired for interest, not indifference

ADHD isn't a lack of attention so much as a different way of regulating it. An ADHD brain tends to be guided by interest, novelty, urgency, and meaning, rather than by importance alone. That's why a task can feel impossible one moment and effortless the next: it isn't about willpower or laziness, it's about how attention and motivation are organised. Hyperfocus, big feelings, creativity, and a mind that makes unexpected connections are all part of that same wiring. The aim here isn't to force yourself into a neurotypical mould, but to understand your brain well enough to work with it.

Tools

Working with your ADHD, not against it

When the task feels too big, shrink the time

When something like getting up and showering feels impossible, you don't have to do the whole thing. Pick the smallest version that matches the energy you actually have right now:

30 seconds One small thing — notice your breath. Just one slow inhale, and a slow exhale.
3 minutes Sing one song, keep the breathing going, or open your Audible app and press play.
13 minutes Something a little bigger — wash your face, or step into the shower.
30 minutes A longer task — cook a simple meal, or stock up on easy groceries so future-you has options ready.

Each step counts as a win on its own. If all you manage today is the 30-second version, that still counts.

Three routines for three kinds of days

You don't have to run the same routine every day. Having a few versions ready means there's always one that fits the capacity you actually have, without the shame.

Minimum day For when getting out of bed feels like a lot. Comfy clothes are completely fine. Food is non-negotiable, but it can be as simple as an Ensure or a protein bar.
Average day Maybe a yogurt and washing your face. Small, doable, and enough.
Fuller day When you've got more in the tank — a shower, a real meal, getting dressed, maybe a short walk.

The point isn't to hit the top tier every day. It's to meet yourself where you are, and then let go of the shame for not doing more.

To-do lists & priorities, ADHD-style

Two small shifts that make lists actually work with your brain instead of against it:

Make it visual Leave the thing you need where you'll actually see it — on the counter, by the door — as a physical cue, instead of trusting yourself to remember it later.
Eat the ice cream The usual advice is “eat the frog” — do the hardest thing first. For an ADHD brain, that can stall you completely. Instead, start with something small or genuinely enjoyable to build momentum, then ride that energy into the harder tasks.

Your top priority doesn't have to be the very first thing you do. Momentum matters more than order.

A few more hacks worth trying

Small shifts that make a big difference

Body doubling

Hard tasks get easier with quiet company. “Body doubling” means doing your task while someone else is simply present — a friend on a video call, someone at the next table, or even a “work with me” video. You're not asking for help; their presence just makes it easier for your brain to stay on track.

Make time visible

ADHD often comes with “time blindness,” where time feels slippery and an hour vanishes without warning. Make it physical: a visual timer you can watch count down, alarms for transitions, and clocks you can actually see. When time is visible, it's far easier to trust and work with.

Pair the boring with the good

Sometimes called temptation bundling: let yourself have a certain treat only while doing the task you avoid. The show you're hooked on plays only while you fold laundry; the fancy coffee comes out only while you answer emails. It gives a dopamine-driven brain a real reason to start.
