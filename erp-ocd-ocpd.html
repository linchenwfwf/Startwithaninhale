ERP for OCD and OCPD, with the part that needs control

Hands-on exposure practice for neurodivergent adults, alongside the care you already have.

Knowing where your need for control comes from is valuable, but it may not stop the checking, the rereading, or the rewriting. And pushing through exposures can feel like a fight with yourself. In these sessions we do both things: we get to know the part of you that needs control, and then we practice, together, doing something different.

Three strands

Recognize it, face it, practice it

Parts work: recognize

We learn to spot the part that takes over: what it does, what it fears, and when it first took the job. Now it's a part of you, rather than all of you.

ERP: face it

Exposure and response prevention means deliberately approaching what OCD warns you about, while not doing the ritual. Your brain learns something new that talking alone rarely teaches.

Practice help: do it

We don't only plan exposures. We do them together, and then we build a practice you can carry on with between sessions.

OCD or OCPD?

Similar names, different relationships to control

The two can look alike, and some people have both. What differs most is how they feel from the inside. We sort this out together, because it shapes the work.

OCDOCPD
How it feelsUnwanted and intrusive. It clashes with who you are.Like who you are. It can feel like high standards or simply the right way.
What drives itFear, doubt, or a “not just right” feeling, relieved briefly by rituals.Rules, order, productivity, and a belief that things must be done properly.
Where it shows upOften specific themes, like contamination, harm, or checking.Across life: work, relationships, money, and rest.
The usual costDistress, and time lost to rituals.Frustration, tense relationships, overwork, trouble relaxing.
What practice targetsFacing the fear without the compulsion.Loosening the rule, and trying flexibility on behalf of what you value.
A note on OCPD. ERP is the most researched therapy for OCD. OCPD has far less research, and cognitive behavioral approaches have the most support so far. ERP wasn't developed for OCPD, so I adapt its ideas as behavioral experiments. If you have both, this matters: one study of people with OCD found that OCPD traits, especially perfectionism, predicted a weaker response to ERP, and its authors suggest addressing perfectionism directly. That's a big part of why we work with this part by name.

Parts work

Who's in charge right now?

In parts work, we treat a need for control as a protector with a job. Clients often give these parts names of their own. A few I might listen for:

The Checker

“Just once more, to be sure.” Locks, emails, stoves, your own memory.

The Perfectionist

“It isn't ready.” Rewrites the message ten times and misses the deadline protecting it.

The Rule-Keeper

“There is a right way.” Keeps the order, and gets upset when others don't.

The Reassurance Seeker

“Are you sure it's okay?” Asks, Googles, and confesses, for relief that fades.

The Doer

“You can rest when it's finished.” It's never finished.

The Reviewer

“Let's replay that conversation.” Solves in your head what can't be solved.

Once we find the part, we get curious. These are questions we might ask it together:

Questions for the part

  • What are you afraid would happen if you stopped?
  • What feeling are you keeping me away from?
  • When did you take this job, and how old were you?

Questions for negotiating

  • What has this job cost you, and me?
  • What would you need in order to step back for ten minutes?
  • Would you be willing to watch while I try something?
Why this isn't reassurance. Parts work can become a ritual if it's used to feel certain. So I won't tell your part that nothing bad will happen, and we don't use a conversation with a part to get rid of anxiety before an exposure. We ask the part to allow the practice. Then we let the discomfort be there, and uncertainty stays uncertain.

Inside a session

What a practice session looks like

  1. Check in with the part. What has it been doing this week? What's it most worried about today?
  2. Choose the practice. You pick from a list we built together. You choose, always. It should feel hard but doable.
  3. Name the prediction. What does the part expect will happen? How sure is it, and what would you do if you couldn't stand it?
  4. Do it, without the ritual. We do it together, in the office or over video. I stay with you and I won't reassure you. You can notice your body and move or stim as you need.
  5. Ask what you learned. What happened, compared with the prediction? We don't need the anxiety to go down for it to count. Today's research points to learning you can tolerate it and that the prediction was off.
  6. Plan the week. We pick one or two practices to repeat at different times and in different places, because learning travels better that way.

Examples

What practice can actually be

These are examples only. Yours will be built around your own fears and values, and nothing here is a test of courage. Exposures aren't meant to be dangerous, and we never choose anything that goes against your values.

OCD practice

  • Lock the door once and walk away
  • Touch a doorknob, then eat a snack without washing
  • Write down the feared thought and read it aloud
  • Say “maybe, maybe not” instead of answering the doubt
  • Delay a ritual by 10 minutes, then longer
  • Don't ask your partner if it's okay

OCPD practice

  • Send the email after one read
  • Leave a small, deliberate typo in place
  • Let someone load the dishwasher their way, and don't redo it
  • Stop a task at the time limit, unfinished
  • Hand off a task and don't check it
  • Spend an hour on something with no purpose

Practice help

Support between sessions

Change happens in the repetitions, and doing exposures alone is hard. Getting stuck is common and doesn't mean you failed.

Practice where it happens

Online sessions let us work in your actual kitchen or at your actual desk. In the office, we can step out and practice in real places nearby.

A ladder you built

We list your triggers and rank them, using my free ERP Exposure Worksheet for your plan, your log, and notes on your protector part.

Troubleshooting

We look for hidden rituals, like mental reviewing or subtle checking, and we adjust when a practice is too big or too small.

People at home

Partners and family often get drawn in by answering questions or following the rules. With your OK, we can teach them how to help.

Neurodivergent-affirming

Not every routine is a compulsion

This is why my neurodivergent focus matters here. ERP that targets the wrong thing can do harm. Autistic adults in one interview study described their repetitive behaviors and focused interests as part of who they are, and OCD as an unwanted intruder, one that can move in on routines and spoil a beloved interest.

What we leave alone

Stimming, routines that keep you regulated, special interests, and sensory needs aren't exposure targets. We start by telling those apart from OCD, together, and we take our time with it.

Clear and concrete

I explain why we're doing each step, in direct language. Written plans, visual ladders, and predictable sessions are all welcome.

Flexible by design

Stimming, fidgets, and comfort items are welcome in session. If shutdown or sensory overload hits, we stop and regulate. That doesn't count against you.

Rules that kept you safe

For many late-diagnosed adults, rigid systems began as a way to survive a confusing world or a demanding home. We honor what they did before asking anything to change.

Questions

Common questions

What does conjunctive mean?

Conjunctive (also called adjunctive) means this work runs alongside the care you already have. Your therapist stays your therapist. I add one focused piece they may not offer, and with your written permission we can stay in touch so the work fits together.

Do you combine IFS and ERP in every session?

It depends on what you need. Some sessions are mostly practice. Some are mostly listening to a part that doesn't want to practice. ERP has strong research behind it. Combining it with parts work is my clinical approach and hasn't yet been tested in controlled trials, so ERP stays the backbone of the work.

Will you make me do something I don't want to do?

No. You choose each step, and you can always stop. I'll be honest if I think a part is steering us away from the practice, but the decision is yours.

My therapist says I have OCD traits, but I'm not sure. Can I still come?

Yes. You don't need a diagnosis to have a consultation. We can look at what's going on, and part of our work might be working out what fits.

I don't feel distressed by my rules, but my partner does. Is this for me?

That's very common with OCPD, and it's a fine place to start. We can begin with what these rules cost you and what you want your life and relationships to feel like.

When is this not the right fit?

Some OCD needs more than weekly sessions, such as when it takes up most of your day or you can't manage work or self-care. If so, I'd help you look for intensive care. Many people also benefit from medication, which you can discuss with a psychiatrist.
